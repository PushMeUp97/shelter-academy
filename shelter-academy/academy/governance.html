Governance

Who decides what — and the policies that need to be in writing before anything else.

⏱ ~22 min read 📋 Includes a self-check checklist 🎯 For trustees, EDs, and managers

What governance covers

Governance is the structure that decides who is allowed to make which decisions, and how those decisions get reviewed. For a shelter, that structure usually sits across two layers: a board (or equivalent oversight body) that sets direction and holds the organization accountable, and staff who run day-to-day operations within the limits the board sets.

Weak governance rarely looks dramatic from the outside. It looks like decisions nobody remembers making, a budget nobody approved in writing, or a policy that exists in one person's memory instead of a shared document. Strong governance is mostly invisible too — it just means there's a clear answer, on file, when someone asks "who decided this, and when?"

Board vs. staff roles

The most common governance failure in small shelters isn't a missing policy — it's an unclear line between what the board decides and what staff decide. The table below is a starting point; your bylaws are the final word.

Typical division of responsibility
AreaBoard's roleStaff's role
Mission & strategySets and reviews itExecutes within it
BudgetApproves the annual budgetManages spending within it
Executive directorHires, evaluates, and can dismissManages all other staff
PolicyApproves major policiesDrafts policies, applies them daily
Client servicesSets standards, doesn't intervene case-by-caseDelivers and adjusts services
Risk & complianceReviews the risk register periodicallyMaintains and acts on it
Why this matters

When a board gets involved in day-to-day case decisions, or staff make strategic commitments without board approval, accountability breaks down in both directions. Neither side can be held responsible for a decision they didn't actually control.

Core policies to have on file

These are the policies that come up first when a funder, auditor, or insurer asks "can we see that in writing?" If your shelter doesn't have one of these yet, it's worth treating as the next board agenda item.

  • Safeguarding / client protection policy — how the organization prevents, recognizes, and responds to harm to the people it serves.
  • Code of conduct — expected behavior for staff, volunteers, and board members, including boundaries with clients.
  • Conflict of interest policy — how board and staff disclose and manage personal interests that could affect their decisions.
  • Whistleblower / concern-raising policy — a protected way for anyone to raise a concern without going through the person it's about.
  • Financial controls policy — who can authorize spending, sign checks, or approve reimbursements, and at what dollar thresholds.
  • Document retention policy — what records get kept, for how long, and how they're destroyed (this also feeds directly into MOD-04).

Meeting cadence & documentation

A board that meets without producing a record is, on paper, indistinguishable from a board that never met at all. Two habits fix most of this:

  • Minutes, not transcripts. Record what was decided and who is responsible for follow-up — not a word-for-word account of the discussion.
  • A standing decisions log. A single running document listing every formal board decision, the date, and the vote, separate from the minutes of any one meeting. This is the fastest way to answer "when did we approve that?"
A reasonable minimum cadence

Full board: quarterly at minimum, monthly if the organization is small enough that quarterly leaves real gaps. Executive committee or chair check-ins with the ED: monthly. Anything involving immediate risk should never wait for the next scheduled meeting — see MOD-03 for escalation outside the regular cycle.
