MOD-04 · TECHNOLOGY & SECURITY

Technology & Security

Protecting client records, and sometimes client location, from the people who shouldn't have access to them.

Why this is different at a shelter

Most organizations protect data to avoid embarrassment or fines. A shelter protects data because, in some cases, exposure can put a client's physical safety at risk — a current address shouldn't reach someone a client is staying away from. That changes the priority order: location and identity confidentiality come before convenience, every time.

Data protection basics

These four controls cover most of what a small shelter needs, without requiring a dedicated IT department.

  • Access controls. Client records are visible only to staff who need them for their role — not the whole staff list by default. Review who has access at least twice a year.
  • Encryption in transit and at rest. Client data sent by email or stored on a shared drive should be encrypted; if your current system can't do this, that's a finding for the risk register, not something to work around informally.
  • A written retention policy. Decide, in advance, how long records are kept after a client leaves and how they're destroyed — paper shredded, digital files permanently deleted — rather than deciding case by case.
  • Unique logins, not shared accounts. A shared login makes it impossible to know who accessed what, which matters as much for protecting staff as clients.

Where digital and physical security meet

Technology security and physical security are usually treated as separate problems. At a shelter, they're the same problem from two angles.

Digital controls and their physical counterpart
Digital controlPhysical equivalent
Access-controlled client databaseLocked file cabinets, restricted-access rooms
Login audit trailSign-in/sign-out log for visitors and staff
Address confidentiality in recordsNo exterior signage revealing the shelter's exact function or location
Camera footage retention policySame retention and destruction rules applied to physical security footage
A quiet but common gap

Visitor logs and camera footage are personal data too, and often get left out of the retention policy written for client case files. If your policy only mentions case records, it likely doesn't yet cover everything that needs it.

Responding to a breach

A breach — digital or physical — follows the same escalation logic as MOD-03, with two additions specific to data.

  • Contain first. Revoke the exposed access (a lost laptop, a compromised login) before doing anything else.
  • Assess what was exposed — specifically whether it included anything that could reveal a client's location to someone they're staying away from. This determines urgency more than the technical scale of the breach.
  • Follow your jurisdiction's breach notification law if personal data was exposed — timelines and requirements vary, so confirm specifics with counsel rather than assuming a default.
  • Report and debrief using the same workflow as MOD-03, with the risk register updated to reflect what allowed the breach to happen.
