Risk Response & Reporting

Four response strategies

Once a risk is identified — through the register in MOD-02 or in the moment — there are only four ways to respond to it. Naming which one you're choosing, and why, is what turns a reaction into a decision.

Choosing a response strategy
StrategyWhat it meansExample
AvoidStop doing the thing that creates the riskDiscontinuing an unsupervised activity that's caused repeated injuries
MitigateReduce the likelihood or impactAdding a second staff member to overnight shifts
TransferShift the financial consequence elsewhereInsurance, contracted security, indemnification clauses
AcceptKnowingly carry the risk because the cost of acting outweighs itA low-likelihood, low-impact risk that's been reviewed and documented as acceptable
The fifth option, which isn't an option

Ignoring a risk because it hasn't been formally accepted is different from accepting it. "Accept" requires a documented decision by someone with the authority to make it — usually the board for high-severity risks, per the roles table in MOD-01.

The incident reporting workflow

An incident is anything that did happen, as opposed to a risk, which is something that could. The sequence matters more than any single step done perfectly.

  • 1. Immediate response. Address safety first — this is the only step that should happen before any documentation begins.
  • 2. Stabilize and contain. Make sure the situation isn't ongoing before stepping back to document it.
  • 3. Document while it's fresh. Write the initial report within hours, not days — memory degrades fast, especially under stress.
  • 4. Escalate per the reporting chain. Notify the right people at the right level — see the table below.
  • 5. Debrief. A short, blame-free review of what happened and whether the risk register or a policy needs to change.

On mandatory reporting

Where a legal mandatory-reporting duty applies, it overrides your internal chain — report to the authority first, then notify leadership. This module is reference material, not legal advice; confirm your jurisdiction's specific requirements with counsel.

Writing a report that holds up

A good incident report separates what was observed from what was inferred, and gives enough detail that someone who wasn't there could understand exactly what happened.

  • Facts, not conclusions. "Client raised their voice and stepped toward staff" — not "client became aggressive."
  • Times, not impressions. Specific timestamps wherever possible, not "early evening."
  • Names and roles of everyone involved or present, including witnesses.
  • Actions taken, in the order they happened, including who was notified and when.
  • No speculation about cause unless it's clearly labeled as the writer's opinion, separate from the factual account.
