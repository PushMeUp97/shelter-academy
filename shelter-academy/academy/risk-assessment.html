Risk assessment, defined plainly

A risk assessment is just a written answer to three questions, repeated for everything that could go wrong: what could happen, how likely is it, and how bad would it be if it did? The output is a risk register — a single document listing each risk, its score, and what's being done about it.

The reason most risk registers stop being used after the first month isn't that the format is wrong. It's that they're built once, during a grant application or audit, and never touched again. A register only earns its place if someone owns it and revisits it on a schedule — see the cadence note in MOD-01.

Common shelter risk categories

Generic corporate risk templates tend to miss the risks that are specific to running a shelter. Start from this list instead, and add what's specific to your site and population.

Risk categories worth a line item
CategoryExamples
Client safeguardingAbuse or exploitation by staff, volunteers, or other clients; inadequate vetting
Physical safetyFire egress, overcrowding, unsecured hazardous materials, violence on-site
StaffingBurnout-driven turnover, single points of failure, inadequate shift coverage overnight
FinancialFunding cliffs, unrestricted vs. restricted fund confusion, fraud
ReputationalA poorly handled incident becoming public, loss of community or funder trust
Data & privacyClient records exposed, location confidentiality broken (see MOD-04)
Legal & regulatoryLicensing lapses, non-compliance with mandatory reporting laws

Scoring likelihood & impact

A simple 1–3 scale on each axis is enough for most shelters — finer scales tend to create false precision without improving decisions. Multiply the two to get a priority score.

Likelihood × impact scoring
ScoreLikelihoodImpact
1Rare — hasn't happened hereMinor — handled within the shift
2Possible — has happened occasionallySerious — requires management involvement
3Likely — has happened recently or repeatedlySevere — safety, legal, or existential consequences
Where honesty matters most

The temptation is to score familiar risks lower because "we've always managed." Familiarity isn't the same as low likelihood — if it's happened before, it's likely, by definition. Score based on history and conditions, not on how comfortable the topic feels to discuss.

Running a walkthrough assessment

The fastest way to populate or refresh a register is a physical and procedural walkthrough, done with more than one person so blind spots get caught.

  • Walk the physical site as if you were a new client and separately as if you were a new staff member on an overnight shift — different routes surface different hazards.
  • Review the last 12 months of incident reports (see MOD-03) for patterns that haven't made it into the register yet.
  • Ask frontline staff directly what they worry about that's never come up in a meeting — they often see risk before it's documented anywhere.
  • Check every policy from MOD-01 against what actually happens day to day; a gap between the two is itself a risk.
